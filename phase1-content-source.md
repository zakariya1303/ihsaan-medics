What Is a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis?
A plain-English introduction to the type of research you are about to do.

Start here: four types of review
The medical literature is full of different kinds of reviews. Here is how they differ.

Type	What it is
Narrative review	A broad overview written by an expert, summarising what is known about a topic. Not reproducible — two people doing the same review might reach different conclusions.
Scoping review	Maps out what research exists on a topic without formally assessing quality. Good for understanding a field, not for answering a specific clinical question.
Systematic review	A reproducible, exhaustive search of the literature to answer one specific, pre-defined question. Every step is documented so another researcher could repeat it. This is what you are doing.
Meta-analysis	A statistical technique used inside a systematic review to pool numerical results from multiple studies into a single combined estimate. Not all systematic reviews include a meta-analysis — and that is fine.

IMPORTANT — NARRATIVE SYNTHESIS IS NOT A FAILURE
Many student reviews end up doing a narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis. This happens when studies are too different from each other to pool statistically. A well-conducted narrative synthesis is a valid, publishable output. Do not drop out if a forest plot does not materialise.

Why systematic reviews matter
A well-conducted systematic review sits at the top of the evidence hierarchy. It tells clinicians, policymakers, and researchers what the totality of published evidence says about a question — not just one study, but all of them. That is why they get published and why they influence clinical guidelines.

As a student, conducting a systematic review teaches you how to ask a precise clinical question, how to navigate medical databases, how to critically appraise evidence, and how to synthesise findings. These skills matter for your career regardless of whether you end up in research.

The infrastructure that makes systematic reviews trustworthy
Two tools underpin almost every modern systematic review. You will use both.

PROSPERO
PROSPERO is the international register for systematic reviews. You register your review before you start searching — this creates a public, time-stamped record of your question, methods, and analysis plan. It prevents outcome-reporting bias (changing your question after you see the results) and stops two teams accidentally doing the same review.
Note: PROSPERO only accepts systematic reviews. It does not accept scoping reviews or pure literature reviews.

PRISMA
PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) is the reporting standard for systematic reviews. The PRISMA 2020 checklist tells you exactly what to include in your manuscript — and journals expect you to follow it. You will also produce a PRISMA flow diagram showing how many records you found, screened, and included.

What you will produce on this programme
•	A registered PROSPERO protocol — your pre-committed question and methods
•	A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram — your audit trail of records found and screened
•	A data extraction sheet — the data you pulled from each included study
•	A risk-of-bias assessment — a judgement of each study's methodological quality
•	A synthesis — either a meta-analysis (pooled statistics) or a structured narrative synthesis
•	A complete manuscript draft — structured to PRISMA 2020, ready for supervisor review and journal submission

Stay current: how to find research ideas
The best systematic review ideas come from gaps you notice in the current literature — and the best way to notice gaps is to stay up to date with what is being published. Here is the habit we recommend you build from Week 1.

FIND YOUR TOP 4 JOURNALS
Identify the four most important journals in your specialty or sub-specialty. These are the journals that publish the studies you would want to include in a review, and where you would want to publish your own work. If you are not sure which journals matter in your field, ask your supervisor — they will know instantly.

Step 1 — Identify your top 4 journals
Think about your specialty. If you are interested in cardiology, that might be the New England Journal of Medicine, JAMA Cardiology, the European Heart Journal, and Heart. If you are in surgery, it might be the British Journal of Surgery, JAMA Surgery, Annals of Surgery, and the Lancet.
A quick way to find the leading journals in any field: search PubMed for a topic you care about, filter by systematic review, and look at where the best recent reviews were published. Those are your target journals.

Step 2 — Subscribe to their table of contents alerts
Every major journal sends a free weekly or monthly email with its latest contents — titles, abstracts, links. This takes two minutes to set up and means the literature comes to you instead of you hunting for it.
18.	Go to each journal's website.
19.	Find "Alerts", "Email notifications", or "Table of Contents" — usually under "For Authors" or in the footer.
20.	Enter your email and subscribe.
You will get an email each time a new issue is published. Scan the titles. Anything that catches your eye, save it to Zotero.

Step 3 — Follow them on X (Twitter)
Most top medical journals are active on X and post new research daily. Following them takes thirty seconds and means your timeline becomes a live feed of what is being published.
Search for your journals on X and follow their official accounts. Examples: @NEJM, @JAMAInternalMed, @TheLancet, @EuropeanHeartJ. Most publish daily highlights, open-access papers, and links to new systematic reviews as soon as they go live.

WHY THIS MATTERS
Staying current is how you spot research gaps — the question that nobody has quite answered yet, or the field where a lot of new primary studies have appeared since the last review. This is where good review ideas come from. Make it a habit from day one.

Critical Appraisal Mini-Guide
How to read a medical paper — and how to spot when something is not right.

Critical appraisal is the skill of reading a study carefully and asking: is this research valid? Are the conclusions justified by the data? Should I trust this result?
You will need this skill throughout the programme — when assessing studies for inclusion in your review, and when conducting formal risk-of-bias assessment in Phase 3. This guide gives you the foundations.

Step 1 — Read the abstract first, then the full paper
Read the abstract to understand what the study claims. Then read the full paper to decide whether those claims are justified. The abstract is written to sell the paper; the Methods section is where the truth lives.

Step 2 — Identify the study design
The study design determines what you can and cannot conclude. The main designs you will encounter in systematic reviews:

Study design	What it tells you
Randomised controlled trial (RCT)	The strongest evidence for whether an intervention works. Participants are randomly assigned to groups, controlling for confounders.
Cohort study	Follows a group of people over time. Good for understanding risk factors and prognosis. Cannot prove causation as reliably as an RCT.
Case-control study	Compares people with a condition to those without, looking backwards for differences in exposure. Useful for rare outcomes. Prone to recall bias.
Cross-sectional study	A snapshot in time. Useful for prevalence data. Cannot establish temporal relationships.
Case report / case series	Describes one or a few patients. Useful for hypothesis generation. Very low on the evidence hierarchy.

Step 3 — Ask the PICO questions
For any study you read, ask:
•	Population: who were the participants, and are they similar to the patients the study claims to apply to?
•	Intervention (or Exposure): what exactly was done to the participants, at what dose, for how long?
•	Comparator: what was it compared to? (placebo, usual care, a different intervention?)
•	Outcome: what was measured, and was it a meaningful outcome or a surrogate?

Step 4 — Assess the risk of bias
Risk of bias refers to the ways a study's design or conduct may have introduced systematic error — making the results less trustworthy. In Phase 3, you will use formal tools (RoB 2 for RCTs, ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies) to do this systematically. For now, learn to spot the major issues:

•	Selection bias: were participants randomised? If not, are the groups comparable at baseline?
•	Performance bias: did participants or researchers know which treatment group they were in? (Blinding)
•	Detection bias: was the outcome measured in the same way in both groups?
•	Attrition bias: how many participants dropped out, and are they accounted for?
•	Reporting bias: were all outcomes reported, or only the significant ones?

Step 5 — Use a CASP checklist
The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) provides free, structured checklists for each study design — RCTs, cohort studies, case-control studies, systematic reviews, and more. They walk you through the key appraisal questions in a logical order.
•	CASP checklists (free download)

QUICK RULE OF THUMB
A study can have an interesting question and still have poor methodology. Always read the Methods section critically. If you cannot tell from the Methods section how something was done, that is itself a problem.

Phase 1 Knowledge Check
10 questions. You need 70% to unlock Gate 1. You can retake as many times as you like.

Q1.  What is the key difference between a systematic review and a narrative review?
A.  A systematic review is longer.
B.  A systematic review uses a pre-defined, reproducible search strategy and transparent methods.
C.  A systematic review is written by more authors.
D.  A systematic review always includes a meta-analysis.
Correct answer: B

Q2.  A meta-analysis is:
A.  Another name for a systematic review.
B.  A statistical method used to pool results from multiple studies within a systematic review.
C.  A type of randomised controlled trial.
D.  A search strategy for finding published papers.
Correct answer: B

Q3.  Your team conducts a systematic review but the included studies are too heterogeneous to pool statistically. What should you do?
A.  Abandon the review — it cannot be published without a forest plot.
B.  Cherry-pick the most similar studies and pool only those.
C.  Conduct a structured narrative synthesis instead — this is a valid publishable output.
D.  Change your eligibility criteria until the studies become homogeneous enough to pool.
Correct answer: C

Q4.  What is PROSPERO?
A.  A database of published systematic reviews.
B.  A reporting guideline for systematic reviews.
C.  An international register where systematic reviews are registered before the search begins.
D.  A screening tool for titles and abstracts.
Correct answer: C

Q5.  Why do you register your review on PROSPERO before searching?
A.  It is required by all journals before submission.
B.  It creates a public time-stamped record of your question and methods, preventing outcome-reporting bias and duplicate reviews.
C.  It gives you access to full-text articles.
D.  It automatically generates a search strategy for you.
Correct answer: B

Q6.  What does the 'P' stand for in PICO?
A.  Protocol
B.  Publication
C.  Population
D.  Primary outcome
Correct answer: C

Q7.  A scoping search (H1) is the same as the full systematic search you will conduct in Phase 3.
A.  True — both involve the same databases and level of detail.
B.  False — a scoping search is a quick 1–2 hour reconnaissance to check your question is not already answered. The full search is exhaustive and built with a librarian.
C.  True — both take the same amount of time.
D.  False — a scoping search is done after registration, not before.
Correct answer: B

Q8.  PRISMA stands for:
A.  Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses.
B.  Primary Research in Systematic Medical Abstracts.
C.  Protocol Registration and Indexing of Systematic Meta-Analyses.
D.  Peer Review in Systematic Medical Assessment.
Correct answer: A

Q9.  Which of the following is the strongest study design for testing whether an intervention works?
A.  Case series
B.  Cross-sectional study
C.  Cohort study
D.  Randomised controlled trial
Correct answer: D

Q10.  You are reading a study and notice that a large number of participants dropped out before the end, and this is not accounted for in the analysis. This is an example of:
A.  Detection bias
B.  Selection bias
C.  Attrition bias
D.  Performance bias
